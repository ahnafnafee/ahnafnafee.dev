On November 6, 2026, I gave a five-minute lightning talk on FlowGate at George Mason University's first School of Computing PhD Research Symposium. It was one of 15 talks selected from 107 submitted abstracts. FlowGate asks a simple question: when two rides on a smart trainer take the same effort, can a cycling game tell which one pulled the rider in? The idea is to use brain sensing only while training a model, so that the finished model can estimate absorption from ordinary trainer and game data. This post covers the idea, the full slide deck with what I said on each slide, and how I presented early-stage work without overstating it.

## The Symposium

The [1st School of Computing PhD Research Symposium](/documents/soc-symposium-2026-program.pdf) ran on a Friday afternoon in the Nguyen Engineering Building. Doctoral students across the School of Computing shared their work as lightning talks or as posters in a digital research gallery on the atrium screens. My talk was T4, the fourth in the afternoon lightning session.

The format was strict. Each speaker had five minutes, there were no questions between talks, and judges scored every talk on presentation, significance, innovation and approach. Attendees also voted for a People's Choice talk. FlowGate also appeared in the digital gallery as a poster, so the work had two forms that day: a talk that had to be heard once and understood, and a poster that people could read at their own pace.

## The Idea Behind FlowGate

Pedaling power tells a game how hard a rider is working. It does not tell the game whether the rider is absorbed in the ride or watching the clock. Absorption, the feeling of being fully caught up in an activity, is one dimension of flow, and it is the experience FlowGate tries to estimate.

The difficulty is that experience is hard to measure during play. Brain sensing can help: functional near-infrared spectroscopy (fNIRS) uses a cap with near-infrared light to estimate changes in blood oxygenation. But nobody wants to wear a sensor cap to play a cycling game at home.

FlowGate's proposed answer is a teacher-student setup. In the lab, a teacher model receives the fNIRS measurements together with trainer and game data and learns to predict how absorbed riders report feeling. A student model learns from the same reports, with the teacher's predictions as extra guidance, but it only ever sees trainer and game data. If the approach works, the cap stays in the lab and the student goes into the game. The idea follows the learning using privileged information framework from Vapnik and Izmailov, where extra information is available during training but not when the model is used.

The prediction target is the rider's own report. During a ride, the game asks how absorbed the rider felt, and the model uses only the trainer and game data recorded before that question appeared.

## Being Honest About Where the Work Is

The hardest part of writing this talk was the findings slide. FlowGate is at the prototype and pre-pilot stage. The cycling prototype runs on the DCXR and GVL cycling platform, and rehearsals have refined how the study collects reports and calibrates workload. Human prediction accuracy and any benefit from teaching are still untested.

So the findings slide shows exactly what the pre-pilot work established and nothing more. One logged interaction shows a question appearing and a response recorded about 11 seconds later, which tells me the report collection works in practice. The workload table makes a methods point: in one preliminary ride, the grade-gain setting, which scales the hill steepness the trainer simulates, fell by a third, while average pedaling power fell by only about 13%. A game setting is not a watt target, so the study measures actual power alongside the setting.
